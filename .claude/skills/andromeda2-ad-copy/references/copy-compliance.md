# Copy compliance — rewrites, claim language and the final check

Load at Step 9 for every deck, and before writing anything in health, supplements, weight, beauty/aging, pet health,
telehealth, money or a special ad category. It adds copy-specific rewrites to `psychology-toolkit.md` §12 (read that
first). It is a working guide drawn from the course material, not legal advice: when a brand sells in a regulated
category, its own approved-claims list and counsel win.

## 1. How Meta reviews an ad (Kell course's Facebook compliance deck)

1. **The ad**: unsubstantiated or sensational claims? implies negative attributes of the viewer? withholds information
   (clickbait)? accurately reflects the product sold on the landing page?
2. **Post-click**: clear product information on the page, no unsubstantiated claims, no withholding ("presell" pages
   must still say what is sold).
3. **Post-conversion**: the product delivered matches what was sold.

So the copy, the page and the product are judged as one chain — the same chain message match optimises for. A claim the
page cannot back is a policy risk and a conversion leak.

## 2. Personal attributes: the rule and rewrites

Meta does not allow ads to assert or imply that the viewer has a personal attribute: health or medical condition
(physical or mental), weight/body, age, financial status, race/ethnicity, religion, sexual orientation, gender identity,
disability, criminal record, voting, name. Questions count ("cannot imply viewers' attributes, even in question form").
"You/your" is fine about non-attribute things ("your morning commute", "your dog"); it is the pairing of "you" with an
attribute that fails.

Ways to keep the same identification (C6 analyst note: first-person story openers carry the same recognition with less
risk):
- **First person (a real person's story)**: "I used to check my temples in every shop window."
- **Third person / the many**: "Most people over 50 who stand all day…", "Many nurses tell us…"
- **The situation, not the person**: "12-hour shifts are hard on feet." / "For people who want to feel lighter on their feet."
- **The desire, not the deficit**: "Help managing weight, with a plan from licensed physicians."

| Non-compliant | Problem | Compliant rewrite |
|---|---|---|
| "Are you overweight?" | asserts weight | "For people who want a weight-management plan they can keep." |
| "Sick of checking your temples in every shop window?" (zone example in the toolkit) | implies hair loss | "I was sick of checking my temples in every shop window." (real person) |
| "Still losing hair? Do this for 2 seconds tonight." (Kell) | implies hair loss | "The 2-second night routine [N] men added for thicker-looking hair." |
| "Your diabetes is getting worse." | condition + "your" | "Support healthy blood-sugar levels as part of a balanced diet." (only if substantiated) |
| "Having trouble losing weight and increasing metabolism?" (deck) | implied attribute | "How [N] foods affect energy through the day." |
| "Your anxiety ends today." | mental-health attribute + cure | "A wind-down routine for calmer evenings." |
| "In debt? Here's how to get out." | financial status | "A simple way to plan the month's spending." |
| "Over 60? You need this." | age attribute | "Made for the way we move after 60." / "Built for active retirees." |

Pet products: the attribute rule protects people, so "your dog" is fine; health-claim rules (§3) still apply to the pet.

## 3. Health, supplement, beauty and pet claim language

- **No disease or cure claims.** Supplements and cosmetics describe the experience and the mechanism, not treatment:
  "supports joint comfort and mobility", not "cures arthritis". The Kell deck goes further: no specific disease names in
  the body copy, "it doesn't matter who mentions them" (a quoted doctor counts); a condition may appear only inside a
  product's name.
- **Soft verbs**: helps, supports, promotes, may, can, enjoy (deck: "avoid definitive claims"). "May be causing", "one of the
  reasons" instead of "the cause".
- **Timeframes**: never "X results in Y days" for health, weight or aging (deck; Blanchard). Attach time to the routine or
  the trial instead: "10 minutes a day", "a 30-night trial", "3 tubs = 90 days". A real customer's own timeline may be
  quoted as their experience with "results vary" on the page. Outside health (delivery, setup, wear time) specific
  timeframes are good specificity: "on in under 10 minutes".
- **Weight words** (deck): "helps manage / stabilise / balance weight", not "weight loss / fat loss". No pounds-lost numbers.
- **Aging words** (deck): avoid "look younger", "anti-aging", "prevents aging", "reverse aging"; prefer "smoother-looking
  skin", "helps skin feel firmer", "over time". Avoid "worn, wrinkled, saggy".
- **Before/after**: no before/after copy or imagery for weight loss, aging or results claims (deck, Blanchard); tell a
  first-person success story instead. Never body-shame; no zoom-ins on body parts.
- **Mechanisms must be true**: the cause is real or a fair simplification, and the product actually does it
  (toolkit §4). Keep the source for every mechanism sentence on file.
- **Supplement claims from the field** (Blueprint Q&A): talk about the pain ("if you're groggy, you might be vitamin
  deficient") but don't say "X product solves this for sure".

## 4. Scare, negative and sensational language

| Avoid | Use instead |
|---|---|
| "These 5 foods will ruin your gut" / "destroy", "kill", "wreck", "worst", "disgusting", "harmful" | "How 5 foods affect digestion" — impact, change |
| "Why aspartame increases death risk" | "How aspartame affects health" |
| "secret", "weird trick", "doctors hate this", "miracle", "breakthrough" | "key", "method", "blueprint", "approach" |
| ALL CAPS headlines, "!!!" | sentence case, one exclamation at most |
| "They don't want you to know…" | name the real reason, plainly |
| fear as the sell ("before it's too late") | the desire and the near-term win; keep PANIC for non-health categories and real risks (fire safety) |

Blanchard on telehealth: "mysterious/curiosity copy ... gets flagged immediately"; "simple, direct messaging beat
clever/cute copy".

## 5. Telehealth and prescription offers (Blanchard, in the Denney bundle; verify against Meta's current policy)

**Always include**: "Free consultation" or "Doctor consultation required"; "Prescription required" or "if medically
appropriate"; "Licensed physicians" / "Board-certified doctors"; a clear CTA (Apply, Get Started, Free Consultation); a
professional, medical tone; the network's required disclaimer.

**Never include**: before/after; pounds-lost numbers; time-based claims; "miracle", "breakthrough", "secret"; a medication
name in the headline (body only); price mentions or discounts; "FDA approved program" (say "FDA-approved medication" only if
true); comparisons to other treatments; targeting a health condition directly.

The account needs the healthcare authorisations the category requires before launch (the source cites LegitScript and
Meta approval); get the offer's approved/prohibited claims list.

## 6. Proof integrity: people, reviews, studies, numbers

- **Real people only.** A first-person story is a real customer's words (review or interview, consent on file), a creator
  who used the product (partnership/whitelisted ads; disclose paid or free product), or the founder. Otherwise write it
  as the brand or in the third person. Never present an AI person as a real customer; disclose AI people where law
  requires it (e.g. New York).
- **No invented authority**: no made-up doctors, nurses, studies, universities, publications or "as seen on" logos.
  Borrowed authority must be a real, named person or body who agreed to it. The advertorial pattern "Doctor reveals…"
  narrated by a patient is out.
- **No fake social proof**: no scripted comment threads or comments from fake profiles (Pellegrini's deck suggests it;
  don't), no invented review counts or ratings. Numbers match the live site on the day the ad ships.
- **Studies**: human studies on the ingredients at similar doses for supplements (Kell/FTC); link the study on the page.
- **Testimonials** carry "results may vary" / "results not typical" on the page; quote them exactly.
- **"Made in USA"** only when nearly everything is made and sourced there.
- **Every number** in the deck is either sourced or a `[REPLACE: …]` placeholder. Grep the deck for `[REPLACE` before handover
  and list what is left.

## 7. Transparency: name the product, close the loop

- Name the product or brand in the primary text, a headline, and the creative (deck: "Include product name in post text,
  headline and in video — transparency"). The first line need not carry it; the ad must.
- Close the loop: "Discover these 5 foods that…" with no answer anywhere is withholding. Give the answer or say plainly
  what the click leads to ("Tap Learn More to see the 2-minute explainer").
- The ad's promise is what the page delivers: same product, same offer, same price.

## 8. Offers, prices, scarcity and links

- Discounts only off a price actually charged before (Kell/FTC). "Was $X" must be real.
- Scarcity and deadlines must be true and honoured: a real end date, real stock, a bonus that really ends (Pellegrini's
  "visible and credible" limit). No static "ends today" timers, no "only 3 left" that never changes.
- "Code auto-applied" only when the link applies it (Shopify `/discount/CODE?redirect=/path`, Evolve CRO).
- Subscriptions: state the terms in the ad or the page, with easy cancellation (Kell/FTC).
- Prices: .99 endings are fine and convert better (Blueprint); never bait with a price the page doesn't show.
- Special ad categories (credit, housing, employment, social issues) must be declared; copy cannot target or exclude by
  protected attributes.

## 9. Final compliance checklist (run on every field: primary texts, headlines, description, site-link labels)

- [ ] No "you/your" + attribute, in statements or questions; first person / third person / situation instead.
- [ ] No disease, cure or treatment claims for non-drug products; soft verbs; no disease names in body copy.
- [ ] No "X results in Y days" for health/weight/aging; no pounds-lost numbers; no before/after language there.
- [ ] No scare, negative or sensational words; no "secret / miracle / breakthrough / weird trick"; no caps lock or "!!!".
- [ ] Every person, quote, review count, rating, study and authority is real and on file; first-person stories have consent.
- [ ] Product/brand named in the ad; no withheld answer; ad, page and product match.
- [ ] Prices, discounts, guarantees and deadlines are real and match the page; scarcity is true.
- [ ] Telehealth/prescription: consultation + prescription language present; no medication name in the headline; no price.
- [ ] Brand's banned words and claims list respected.
- [ ] Meta's AI text options ("text improvements", "generate variations", "optimized text per person") reviewed or off in
      regulated categories — they can rewrite vetted wording.
- [ ] No `[REPLACE` left in what is about to be pasted into Ads Manager.

## 10. Source conflicts and the defaults chosen

- **Specific timeframes (Pellegrini, toolkit) vs "cannot claim X results in Y days" (Kell deck, Blanchard)** → specific
  timeframes everywhere except health/weight/aging outcomes, where time attaches to the routine or a real customer's
  quoted experience.
- **Study-based % in telehealth body copy ("15-20% of body weight", allowed per Blanchard's later doc) vs "no specific
  weight-loss numbers" (his checklist)** → follow the stricter checklist unless the network's approved-claims list allows
  the figure.
- **Telehealth angles "fraction of the cost", "better results than…" (Blanchard's "what's working") vs his own "no price,
  no comparisons"** → no price, no comparisons.
- **Pellegrini's fake-profile comments** → never.
- **Advertorial "Doctor Reveals" headlines narrated by patients, "INSIDER" mastheads, static countdowns (C6)** → keep the
  structure and emotion, drop the fakes.
